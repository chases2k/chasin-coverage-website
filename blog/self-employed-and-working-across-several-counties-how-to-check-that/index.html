Self-Employed and Working Across Several Counties? How to Check That Your Health Insurance Plan Goes Where You Do

Chase TaborLicensed health insurance agent
November 22, 20266 min read

The short answer

If your business takes you from county to county, whether a health insurance plan works for you depends on two things you can check before you buy. The first is the insurance plan's service area: the place where it takes members, and for insurance plans that limit your doctors, the place where you get routine care. The second is its network: the doctors and hospitals that have a deal with it. Look up both for the county where you live and the counties where you work. Emergencies follow different rules, and federal and Texas laws add protection there.

Two maps decide where your insurance plan works

HealthCare.gov defines a service area as "A geographic area where a health insurance plan accepts members if it limits membership based on where people live." For insurance plans that limit which doctors and hospitals you may use, "it's also generally the area where you can get routine (non-emergency) services."

The second map is the network: "The facilities, providers and suppliers your health insurer or plan has contracted with to provide health care services." In network means a doctor is on that list. Out of network means they are not.

If you live in Magnolia and drive into Houston for jobs, you go from Montgomery County into Harris County most days. Check both maps for both counties, plus any others where you spend real time.

Your type of insurance plan decides what happens outside the network

Our post on HMO vs PPO explains the letters in detail. Here is what matters when you work on the move.

An HMO (health maintenance organization) is the strictest. HealthCare.gov's page on insurance plan types says "It generally won't cover out-of-network care except in an emergency." It adds: "An HMO may require you to live or work in its service area to be eligible for coverage."

An EPO (exclusive provider organization) works much the same way. The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) puts it simply: "EPOs are like HMOs without any out-of-network coverage." HealthCare.gov notes the exception is an emergency.

A PPO (preferred provider organization) gives you more room. HealthCare.gov says you can see providers outside the network "without a referral for an additional cost." That freedom has limits. TDI says "PPOs have higher premiums but more flexibility in doctors." A premium is the monthly price of your insurance plan. Out of network, you can also get a balance bill: the provider bills you for the difference between their charge and the most your insurance plan will pay.

Routine care is where county lines matter most

Checkups and sick visits are routine care. For insurance plans that limit your doctors, that care generally happens inside the service area. So where you would go for routine visits matters more than where you happen to work on a given day.

Urgent care sits in the middle. HealthCare.gov describes urgent care as care for something "serious enough that a reasonable person would seek care right away, but not so severe it requires emergency room care." When you are two counties from home, that is the visit most likely to catch you off guard. Before you buy, find an in-network urgent care clinic near each place you work often.

TDI also says to "ask if your doctors and specialists are in their network and check that your prescription medications are covered." HealthCare.gov counts pharmacies as part of an insurance plan's network, so check the pharmacies you use on the road too.

Emergencies get extra protection, with limits

HealthCare.gov defines emergency services as "Evaluation of an emergency medical condition and treatment to keep the condition from getting worse." Emergencies are the exception HMOs and EPOs make for out-of-network care. In a real emergency, go to the nearest emergency room.

Federal law helps with the bill. CMS (the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) says "The No Surprises Act protects you from unexpected out-of-network medical bills for emergency room visits." The law went into effect on January 1, 2022, and it "applies to most types of health insurance."

Texas has its own law. TDI says "Patients get surprise medical bills if they get care outside their health plan's network without realizing it." The Texas law bans surprise bills "In emergencies."

The limits matter. TDI says you could still get a bill for your usual share, such as your deductible, which is what you pay before your insurance plan starts to pay. TDI also says "The law doesn't limit your options or change your health plan's coverage for out-of-network care." So these laws don't turn a routine out-of-network visit you chose into an in-network one.

Run this check before you buy

  1. Write down the county where you live and each county where you work most weeks.
  2. Ask if the insurance plan's service area includes your home county, and how it handles routine care in the others.
  3. Look up your doctors in the provider directory, the insurance plan's list of in-network doctors.
  4. Find one in-network urgent care clinic near each place you work often.
  5. Read the Summary of Benefits and Coverage, a short summary of what an insurance plan covers. TDI says "This document lets you make a side-by-side comparison of the coverage in different plans."

If you move your home base, call your insurance plan first. HealthCare.gov's service area page says "The plan may end your coverage if you move out of the plan's service area."

FAQ

Will my health insurance plan cover me in a county where I work but don't live?

It depends on the insurance plan. For insurance plans that limit your doctors, HealthCare.gov says the service area is generally where you get routine care. Check the network in each county where you work before you buy.

Can I get a surprise bill after an out-of-network emergency room visit?

Federal law protects you from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency room visits, and Texas law bans surprise bills in emergencies. You can still owe your usual share, such as your deductible.

Is a PPO always the right pick if I drive a lot for work?

Not always. A PPO lets you see providers outside the network for an added cost, but TDI says PPOs have higher premiums. Compare your real routes and doctors with each insurance plan's network first.
