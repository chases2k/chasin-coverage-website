Self-Employed With Income That Changes Month to Month? How to Choose a Health Insurance Plan You Can Keep

Chase TaborLicensed health insurance agent
November 20, 20266 min read

The short answer

When your pay goes up and down, choose your health insurance plan by your slow months, not your best ones. Pick a monthly price you can still pay when work is quiet. Then make sure you could cover the deductible from money you set aside in busy months. If you use Marketplace help, know that it is based on a guess of your yearly income. A better year than you guessed can mean paying some of that help back at tax time.

Your monthly price has to fit your slowest month

HealthCare.gov defines the premium as "The amount you pay for your health insurance every month." It also calls it "A monthly amount you pay for coverage whether you get services or not."

That second part matters when your income is uneven. The bill comes every month, busy or not.

A simple way to start: look back over the past year of money coming into your business. Find your slowest month. The monthly price of your insurance plan should still feel safe in that month, next to your other bills.

A lower monthly price usually means a higher deductible

A deductible is what you pay for covered care before your insurance plan starts to pay. HealthCare.gov's deductible page puts the trade plainly: "Generally, plans with lower monthly premiums have higher deductibles. Plans with higher monthly premiums usually have lower deductibles."

Its premium page adds a warning: "the plan with the lowest monthly premium may not be the best match for you. If you need much health care, a plan with a slightly higher premium but a lower deductible may save you a lot of money."

With uneven income, ask yourself two questions, not one. Can you pay the monthly price in a slow month? And could you pay the deductible if you got sick or hurt in that same slow month? If the second answer is no, put some money aside in your good months until the deductible is covered.

The out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling. HealthCare.gov's glossary calls it "The most you have to pay for covered services in a plan year." That ceiling has limits. It doesn't include your monthly premiums. It also leaves out "Anything you spend for services your plan doesn't cover" and "Out-of-network care and services." Out of network means doctors and hospitals that don't have a deal with your insurance plan. Our post on deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum walks through both numbers.

Regular care may be covered before the deductible

HealthCare.gov's preventive care page says most health insurance plans must cover a set of preventive services, such as screening tests, at no cost to you. There is a limit: "Generally, these services are covered at no cost to you when provided by an in-network medical provider." In network means the doctor has a deal with your insurance plan.

Other regular care works differently from one insurance plan to the next. The deductible page says "Many plans pay for certain services, like a checkup or disease management programs, before you've met your deductible. Check your plan details."

So ask before you buy: does this insurance plan help pay for regular doctor visits before the deductible, or only after?

Marketplace help is based on a guess of your income

This part is background: Chase Tabor works with private insurance plans, not Marketplace insurance plans.

When you apply on the Marketplace, HealthCare.gov explains, "you estimate your expected income for the year." Your monthly help is based on that guess. HealthCare.gov's page on income changes says that if your income goes up and you don't report it, "you'll have to pay the difference back between the amount you used and the amount you actually qualify for when you file your federal tax return."

The IRS says this "will result in either a smaller refund or a larger balance due."

For a self-employed person, guessing a full year of income ahead of time is hard. One big job late in the year can change the math.

Under the health care law, your income doesn't set the price

For insurance plans under the health care law, HealthCare.gov's page on how premiums are set says "insurance companies can account for only 5 things when setting premiums." In plain words:

  • Your age
  • Where you live
  • Tobacco use
  • Whether the insurance plan covers just you, or also a spouse or children
  • The insurance plan's category, which is how you and the insurance company share costs

Your income is not on the list. A busy month or a slow month doesn't move the price.

The limit: age is on the list. HealthCare.gov says "Premiums can be up to 3 times higher for older people than for younger ones." Before you buy any insurance plan, ask what can change its price when it renews.

An HSA lets you save more in a good month

A health savings account (HSA) is a savings account for medical costs that comes with tax breaks. It works only with a high deductible health plan (HDHP), an insurance plan with a higher deductible that meets IRS rules.

IRS Publication 969 says "The contributions remain in your account until you use them." With uneven income, you can put more in after a strong month and use it for your deductible later.

The limits: Publication 969 says you must be covered by an HDHP on the first day of the month, with no other health coverage except what the rules allow. The IRS also caps how much you can put in each year. Our post on how a health savings account works when you're self-employed has the details.

FAQ

Does my income change the price of my health insurance?

Not for insurance plans under the health care law. HealthCare.gov lists only 5 things insurance companies can use to set premiums, and income isn't one. Marketplace help is different: it is based on your income.

What if I earn more than I guessed while I get Marketplace help?

You may have to pay some of it back. HealthCare.gov says you'll pay back the difference between what you used and what you actually qualify for when you file your federal tax return.

Will my insurance plan pay for a checkup before I meet my deductible?

Often, for preventive care. HealthCare.gov says these services are generally covered at no cost to you when an in-network medical provider gives them. For other visits, check the details of your insurance plan.
