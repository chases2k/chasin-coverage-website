Is a Health Care Sharing Ministry the Same as Health Insurance? What Self-Employed Texans Should Know

Chase TaborLicensed health insurance agent
November 18, 20266 min read

The short answer

No. In Texas, a health care sharing ministry is not health insurance. The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) calls it "a non-insurance arrangement where members agree to make monthly payments to pay for other members' medical expenses." The big difference shows up when a large bill arrives. TDI says: "If an HCSM doesn't pay a member's claim, the member has no legal protection." Discount health cards and subscription health plans aren't insurance either. Each one can have a place, but only if you know what it leaves out before you count on it.

Lower monthly costs are why many people look at these options

When you work for yourself, no employer pays part of your health coverage. So a lower monthly cost is hard to ignore. TDI sees it too: "Some consumers look at alternative health plans to save money. These plans might not be state regulated and don't have to follow federal rules."

TDI then puts a few of these options in a group of their own. "Texas law allows some other ways to pay for health coverage that are exempt from most state regulations and federal protections. These aren't insurance." It adds a warning worth reading twice: "Because they're exempt from regulation, TDI usually can't help if you have a complaint."

Chase Tabor works with private insurance plans. This post is about the options that are not insurance at all, so you can tell the two apart.

A sharing ministry is members sharing bills, not a company paying them

TDI says health care sharing ministries "limit membership to people of a similar faith." Each month, members send in money. That money goes toward other members' medical bills.

TDI points out some limits to weigh:

  • Some ministries "spend a high percentage of member contributions on administrative expenses," which means the cost of running the group.
  • They "often exclude preexisting conditions" (health problems you had before you joined).
  • They "aren't regulated by state or federal government."

TDI also says "Texas has taken enforcement actions against some HCSMs for Texas exemption law violations."

A sharing ministry fits some families and not others

A sharing ministry may suit a healthy family that shares the group's faith. That family should also have enough savings to pay a large bill on its own if the group says no.

It is a poor fit for anyone with an ongoing health problem, since TDI says these groups often leave preexisting conditions out. It is also a poor fit if you need a legal right to have your bills paid. With a sharing ministry, you don't have one.

Discount health cards lower some prices but pay nothing

TDI says discount health plans "typically sell cards that provide members with discounts for services from participating health care doctors and facilities." You show the card and get a lower price at the places that take it.

Here is the catch. TDI says these cards "might use the term 'PPO' (preferred provider organization) to describe their provider network, but they aren't traditional health plans. Members pay the full discounted cost of medical services."

A PPO is a type of insurance plan with its own list of doctors. Seeing those three letters on a card doesn't make the card insurance. We explain the real thing in HMO vs PPO: The Difference in Plain Words for Self-Employed People and Families.

A discount card can be a handy extra. It is not a stand-in for coverage if you end up in the hospital.

Subscription health plans cover doctor visits, not the hospital

TDI says "Subscription health plans often charge members monthly or annual fees to visit a doctor or use telemedicine. These are also called direct care or concierge care." For a busy business owner, easy access to a doctor by phone or video can be worth a lot.

The limit is big, though. TDI says "These plans don't cover hospital care," and "If you need care that the plan doesn't cover, you will have to pay the full cost yourself." So a subscription health plan works best next to an insurance plan, not in place of one.

Check what you are buying before you pay

TDI gives a simple clue: "Alternative health plans will have text on them telling you that they're not traditional health insurance." Read the papers before you sign up, and look for that notice.

Then check who you are dealing with. TDI lets you look up an agent or a company online, and its Help Line can tell you if a company is licensed.

TDI's shopping tips also list warning signs that you might want to move on:

  • "You feel pressured to decide right away. There are no limited time offers or special deals in health insurance."
  • "A price that is much lower than other companies you've checked. This probably means the plan has fewer benefits and more limits."
  • "The agent or salesperson can't answer basic questions about the plan."

Some self-employed families also compare private insurance plans. Those are insurance, and they come with limits of their own. We walk through them in Earn Too Much for Marketplace Help? Health Insurance Options for Self-Employed Families.

FAQ

Is a health care sharing ministry insurance in Texas?

No. TDI calls it a non-insurance arrangement where members agree to make monthly payments toward other members' medical bills.

Will a sharing ministry pay for a health problem I already have?

Often not. TDI says health care sharing ministries often exclude preexisting conditions.

Can TDI help if a sharing ministry won't pay my bill?

Usually not. TDI says it usually can't help with complaints about these exempt options, and that a member has no legal protection if a ministry doesn't pay a claim.

Is a discount card that says PPO the same as a PPO insurance plan?

No. TDI says discount plans might use the term PPO, but they aren't traditional health plans, and members pay the full discounted cost of care.

Does a subscription health plan cover a hospital stay?

No. TDI says subscription health plans don't cover hospital care.
